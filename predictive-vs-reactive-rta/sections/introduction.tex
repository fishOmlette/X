\section{The question}
\label{sec:introduction}

A prediction can tell a robot to act before something goes wrong.  But a simple
threshold can also make it act early: increase the buffer and intervene while
there is still room to recover.  I wanted to understand when prediction adds
something beyond choosing a more conservative threshold.

Runtime assurance provides the setting for this question.  A runtime layer
monitors a safety, performance, or mission condition and intervenes during
execution.  Examples include modular runtime assurance for robotics
\cite{desai2019soter}, mission-level assurance for autonomous driving
\cite{tsai2026missionrta}, monitoring across robot swarms
\cite{kekatos2026swarmrta}, and predictive verification, enforcement, and
monitoring
\cite{pinisetty2017prv,pinisetty2017pre,yoon2019prm,wheatman2021radics}.
Earlier intervention may give recovery enough time to preserve the mission
instead of requiring an emergency stop.

Prediction also has costs.  The monitor has to estimate how fast a margin is
shrinking from noisy, possibly delayed observations and finish within the
robot's decision cycle.  Comparing it only with a reactive rule that waits
until the margin is exhausted gives prediction an easy baseline.  I instead
compare it with a fixed-margin rule that has the same tuning budget.  The
question is: \emph{when does predicting the loss of feasibility outperform
reacting to the current margin?}

\subsection*{What I tried and what I found}

I start with a simplified timing model (\cref{sec:analysis}).  The predictive
trigger studied here turns out to be a current-margin threshold whose buffer
scales with the estimated rate of decline.  Earlier action changes the recovery
outcome only within a band set by recovery duration and deadline slack.  Old
observations consume that lead one for one.  The smallest predictive horizon
wastes less lead than the smallest safe fixed buffer when recovery needs a
similar amount of time across cases but, because rates differ, a different
amount of margin.

I then test the mechanism in two simulation setups (\cref{sec:method,sec:results}).
Exploratory sweeps show where behavior changes.  Separate tuning and testing
runs use different noise seeds and give both rules equal tuning budgets.  I
check failures before comparing completion times.

The three-robot shared-zone test gives the clearest result: both tuned rules
succeed in all 1,620 conflict episodes, while prediction reduces mean completion
time by $2.779$\,s and avoids most unnecessary serializations on clear paths.
The first intervention commits the robots to one crossing order, so choosing
when to commit directly affects how long they wait.

The two-robot test helps explain the other side of the tradeoff.  The tuned
fixed rule records $0/6750$ conflict collisions and the predictive rule records
$2/6750$, both in the slowest-recovery, late-reveal corner of the grid.  I examine
that behavior alongside the timing model to understand how rate estimation
changes the lead actually available for recovery.
