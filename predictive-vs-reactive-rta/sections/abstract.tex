\begin{abstract}
Predictive runtime assurance can trigger recovery before a monitored margin is exhausted. A fixed current-margin rule can also act early, however, by using a larger threshold. We analyze when prediction offers something distinct. In an idealized timing model, a trigger based on projected time-to-infeasibility is a reactive threshold whose margin scales with the estimated degradation rate. Early action changes the recovery outcome only within a band determined by recovery duration and remaining slack. An advantage over one fixed margin across cases further requires degradation rates that vary and can be estimated. Exploratory kinematic sweeps examine this boundary using in-sample comparator selection; separate evaluations use seed-disjoint tuning and testing. In the single-margin study, the searches select $\rho=2.5$ and $T_h=2.5$\,s. The fixed rule records no collisions in 6,750 held-out conflict episodes, whereas the predictive rule records two, so we make no inferential comparison of elapsed time. In a redesigned three-robot study, untreated conflict trajectories collide in all 1,620 held-out runs. Both selected rules complete all 1,620 without collision or headway violation. Conditional on the fixed scenario grid, prediction reduces mean successful makespan by $2.779$\,s (paired 95\% bootstrap interval $[2.659,2.902]$\,s). The findings apply to these simulators and do not establish real-world safety or a general policy ranking. ROS~2/Nav2 validation is reserved for v2.
\end{abstract}
