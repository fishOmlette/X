\begin{abstract}
Predictive runtime assurance triggers recovery before a monitored safety margin
is exhausted, but a reactive rule can also act early by using a larger fixed
margin.  We ask when prediction offers something that a tuned fixed margin
cannot.  In an idealized timing model, a trigger that fires when the projected
time to margin expiry falls below a horizon is a current-margin threshold whose
pad scales with the estimated degradation rate.  Earlier action changes the
recovery outcome only within a band set by recovery duration and deadline
slack, and observation age consumes that band exactly as lost lead does.  The
minimal predictive horizon wastes less lead than the minimal safe fixed pad
when the lead that recovery requires is homogeneous across cases while the
corresponding margin is not, which requires degradation rates that vary and can
be estimated.  We examine these conditions in kinematic simulations with equal
tuning budgets and seed-disjoint tuning and testing.  In a two-robot study, the
tuned fixed rule records no collisions in 6,750 held-out conflict episodes and
the tuned predictive rule records two, so we draw no inference about elapsed
time.  In a three-robot shared-zone study whose untreated trajectories always
collide, both tuned rules succeed in all 1,620 held-out conflict episodes;
prediction shortens mean makespan by $2.78$\,s (paired 95\% bootstrap interval
$[2.66,2.90]$\,s) and serializes far fewer clear paths.  The results are
conditional on the simulated scenario grids and do not establish real-world
safety.
\end{abstract}
