\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{When to Predict}

Before building a predictive assurance layer, one can ask whether its lead would
change what recovery can accomplish.  The analysis suggests four questions.
First, does recovery need lead at all, that is, does some case have
$\tau_r>\delta$?  If not, an unpadded reactive rule suffices
(\cref{prop:boundary}).  Second, how old are the observations?  Age consumes
lead one for one, so reducing latency in the monitoring path can be as valuable
as prediction (\cref{cor:age}).  Third, is the recovery requirement homogeneous
in time or in margin?  If recovery needs a similar lead in every case while
degradation rates differ, the predictive horizon wastes less lead than any safe
fixed pad; if the required margin is similar instead, a fixed pad suffices
(\cref{prop:homogeneity}).  Fourth, can the rate be estimated quickly and
accurately enough from the available observations?  \Cref{prop:reduction} also
suggests an intermediate design: scale a current-margin threshold with an online
rate estimate instead of constructing a full predict-then-recover stack.  Its
value would still depend on estimator delay, noise, and changes in the
degradation rate.

\subsection{Interpreting the Two Studies}

The two simulators produce different results.  In the single-margin study, the
exploratory figures favor prediction over the unpadded rules and, in a few
settings, over the in-sample fixed pad.  After equal-budget, seed-disjoint
tuning, the predictive rule intervenes less often on clear paths and has a lower
descriptive successful-only elapsed time, but it also records two held-out
conflict collisions where the fixed rule records none, so no efficiency
comparison at matched outcomes is available.  Both collisions occurred in the
slowest-recovery, late-reveal corner of the grid ($a_{\max}=0.5$, $p=0.7$),
where the margin decline steepens as the conflict approaches.  A slope fitted
over the eight most recent samples then lags the current rate and shortens the
realized lead, which is consistent with the exact-rate assumption of
\cref{prop:reduction} being violated where it matters most.

In the coordination study, both tuned rules succeed throughout the held-out
conflict grid, and the predictive rule finishes earlier and serializes far fewer
clear paths.  This pattern is consistent with \cref{prop:homogeneity} when
degradation rates vary across robot pairs and scenarios: a fixed pad that is
safe for the fastest degradation must trigger early, and often needlessly, when
degradation is slow or absent.  The result depends on the recovery
design.  The first trigger commits one total order, after which blockers and
release events determine the motion; prediction changes when this commitment
occurs, but the order is never revised.  The paired interval describes variation
over measurement noise on the fixed 27-scenario grid and says nothing about
unseen geometries, robot counts, dynamics, or online replanning.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The evidence comes from controlled two-dimensional point-kinematic simulators
with scalar monitored margins, fixed scenario grids, Gaussian measurement noise,
and one braking or serialization recovery family.  It does not cover vehicle
dynamics, perception failures, localization, networking, asynchronous
execution, observation delay, or a full navigation stack.  The analytical
stopping deadline is deliberately conservative, while the $8.8$\,s replay
deadline is empirical for one deterministic episode and a $0.1$\,s trigger grid.
The exploratory figures select the fixed pad in-sample and keep the horizon at
$2.0$\,s.  The seed-disjoint evaluations hold out noise realizations only; the
simulators, factor levels, and selection rules are fixed rather than sampled
from a target population, and zero observed collisions are not a safety
guarantee.  The coordination model has three robots, one shared zone, and an
immutable one-shot ordering.  The related-work search was targeted rather than
systematic.

\subsection{Future Work}

Three extensions follow directly.  First, injecting observation delay into both
simulators would test \cref{cor:age} and quantify how latency trades against
predictive lead.  Second, varying recovery durations jointly with degradation
rates would test \cref{prop:homogeneity} by measuring the required lead and
margin per case.  Third, experiments on ROS~2 with the Nav2 navigation stack,
with localization degradation, communication faults, planner interactions, and
full-stack telemetry, would test whether the boundary survives realistic
estimation and middleware timing.
