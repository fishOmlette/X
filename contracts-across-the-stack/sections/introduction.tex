\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Multi-robot systems are assembled from components whose correctness claims are
made under different assumptions.  A mission synthesizer may guarantee a
temporal goal under a declared context; a planner may report feasibility for a
particular state and model; middleware may report a quality-of-service event;
and a safety filter may guarantee local invariance under a dynamics and timing
model.  Mission success depends on each claim and on whether its assumptions
remain satisfied when another component or robot consumes its output.

The practical requirement is simple: a result should cross an interface with
enough context for the receiver to decide whether it remains valid.

This interface problem is becoming more important as multi-robot stacks combine
classical task allocation and motion coordination
\cite{gerkey2004mrta,choi2009cbba,sharon2015cbs} with language-conditioned
planning and learned prediction
\cite{mandi2023roco,obata2024lipllm}.  These learned components
expand capability, but they do not replace explicit physical, temporal, or
deployment assumptions.  At lower layers, ROS~2 and DDS transport state and
commands, while runtime assurance and barrier-based controllers may alter a
nominal decision before actuation
\cite{macenski2022ros2,omg2015dds,desai2019soter,wang2017barrier}.  A complete
execution argument must therefore cross semantic, physical, software,
communication, and safety boundaries.

Multi-robot coupling makes this problem distinct from ordinary component
integration.  Stale or ambiguous state at robot $r_i$ can cause a locally
admissible decision that invalidates robot $r_j$'s route, resource claim, or
safety margin.  Join, leave, restart, and network partition events can change
the set of valid assignments even when every message remains syntactically
well-formed.  The relevant interfaces are both vertical, between functions in
one robot, and horizontal, between robots or distributed components.

Existing work addresses parts of this problem through quantitative mission
assurance, contract-based mission design, ROS component contracts,
compositional reasoning for interconnected dynamics, runtime monitoring, and
safety-controller switching
\cite{chaki2013assurance,chaki2013probabilistic,porter2013reliability,
street2023formal,garg2024safecontrol}.  The approaches protect different objects
and produce different evidence.  This survey asks whether their combined
coverage supports the full multi-robot command-to-action path.  More
specifically, I ask:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which objects, assumptions, and guarantees are represented by existing
    contract-based and runtime-assurance approaches?
  \item How are those claims composed, monitored, enforced, or repaired, and
    what evidence supports them?
  \item Which obligations remain implicit when information crosses semantic,
    physical, distributed, and inter-robot boundaries?
\end{enumerate}

The paper makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a cross-layer taxonomy of contract-based assurance organized by the
    object of reasoning, enforcement mechanism, and reported evidence;
  \item a coverage analysis of recurring gaps in temporal validity,
    uncertainty and provenance, implementation and lifecycle compatibility, and
    distributed authority; and
  \item an edge-contract reference framework and failure-attribution taxonomy
    synthesized from those gaps.
\end{enumerate}

The proposed framework is a reference model; the paper does not claim a new
contract algebra or a demonstrated reliability improvement.  \Cref{sec:scope}
defines the review method, \cref{sec:background} states the interface problem,
and \cref{sec:related-work} surveys the literature.  \Cref{sec:gaps} derives the
cross-layer gaps, after which \cref{sec:synthesis} presents the reference
framework.  \Cref{sec:discussion} discusses limitations and open problems.
