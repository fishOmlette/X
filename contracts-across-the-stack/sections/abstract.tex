\begin{abstract}
Multi-robot systems turn mission intent into physical action through planners,
learned predictors, middleware, and runtime safety mechanisms, each of which
offers guarantees under its own assumptions.  When one component's guarantee
becomes another component's assumption, the information needed to check that
assumption is often lost at the interface.  This paper reviews contract-based
and runtime-assurance approaches relevant to multi-robot execution and organizes
them by the object they protect: mission intent, component behavior, dynamical
interaction, distributed state, runtime authority, and embodied execution.  A
coverage analysis identifies five recurring interface gaps: semantic and
physical validity; temporal validity of distributed state; uncertainty,
provenance, and evidence; implementation and lifecycle compatibility; and
intervention and recovery authority.  Public failure reports from the
continuous-integration pipeline of the ROS~2 navigation stack illustrate the
compatibility and lifecycle gap.  From these gaps, we derive an
interface-contract reference framework that distinguishes intra-robot from
inter-robot interfaces and attaches a typed payload, metadata, assumptions,
guarantees, validators, evidence, and a recovery rule to every admitted transfer
under three-valued admission semantics.  For peer state used in collision
avoidance, we derive an admission margin that makes event time, clock error, and
estimation error necessary interface fields and that quantifies when an on-board
estimate is preferable to a more accurate remote one.  A failure taxonomy
separates fault origin, first observation, effect, and recovery authority.  The
framework organizes obligations; it is neither a contract calculus nor a
validated reliability mechanism.
\end{abstract}
