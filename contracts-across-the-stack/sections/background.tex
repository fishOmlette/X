\section{The Multi-Robot Interface Problem}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Coupled Execution}

A multi-robot system contains a team $\mathcal R=\{r_1,\ldots,r_n\}$ whose
objectives, observations, actions, or resources are coupled
\cite{gerkey2004mrta,street2023formal,gielis2022communications}.  The coupling
may arise from a shared mission, collision constraints, task precedence, limited
resources, or communication over a time-varying graph.  Local correctness is
therefore insufficient in general: a decision that is safe for one robot under
an old peer state can violate a joint constraint under the current team state.

Let $x_i$, $u_i$, and $w_i$ denote the state, input, and disturbance of robot
$r_i$.  A general model is
\begin{equation}
  \dot x_i=f_i(x_i,u_i,w_i),\qquad
  g_{ij}(x_i,x_j)\ge 0,\quad \{i,j\}\in\mathcal E_c,
  \label{eq:coupled-dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal E_c$ indexes the coupled team constraints.  For collision
avoidance, $g_{ij}=\lVert p_i-p_j\rVert^2-d_{\min}^2$ for positions $p_i,p_j$
and minimum separation $d_{\min}$; other instances encode formation, visibility,
or connectivity constraints
\cite{sharf2021dynamical,chen2019invariance,wang2017barrier}.  The local
dynamics in \eqref{eq:coupled-dynamics} can be modeled correctly while the joint
constraint is evaluated with an invalid peer state.  This is the mechanical
reason why horizontal interfaces need explicit timing and uncertainty
assumptions.

A command typically passes through task interpretation, allocation, prediction,
motion or action planning, distributed communication, runtime validation, and
physical execution.  These functions need not occupy separate processes or
robots, but each transformation changes the object being reasoned about.  A
language instruction becomes a task model, a task model becomes an assignment,
an assignment becomes trajectories, and distributed observations become inputs
to safety-critical control.  Evidence that is meaningful at one stage may be
insufficient at the next.

\subsection{Contracts, Verification, and Assurance}

An assume--guarantee contract separates an environment assumption $A$ from a
component guarantee $G$ \cite{benveniste2007contracts}.  A contract theory
additionally defines satisfaction, compatibility, refinement, composition, and
quotient for a chosen behavioral model.  Robotics uses the idea at several
levels: a mission contract describes temporal goals and contexts, a component
contract specifies software behavior, a dynamical contract constrains input and
output trajectories, and a runtime-assurance specification governs the transfer
of control authority.  These contract objects are related but not
interchangeable.

I use three terms consistently.  A \emph{contract} states obligations,
\emph{verification} checks a stated property, and \emph{assurance} is the
broader argument and evidence supporting confidence in system behavior.  A
contract contributes to assurance only when its assumptions, checking mechanism,
and evidence are explicit.  An interface schema is therefore not automatically
a contract.  Names, types, time stamps, and version fields become contractual
only when they appear in explicit assumptions or guarantees, are checked by a
defined validator, and have a specified consequence when the check fails.
Likewise, a runtime monitor observes only the properties encoded in its
specification; it cannot infer an omitted frame, validity horizon, or recovery
policy.

\subsection{Kinds of Evidence}
\label{sec:evidence-kinds}

Because every guarantee is conditional on its assumptions, the kind of
supporting evidence matters.  I distinguish three kinds.  \emph{Capability
evidence} shows that a system performs a task.  \emph{Assurance evidence}
supports a specified property under declared assumptions, for example through
proof, synthesis, monitoring, or systematic testing.  \emph{Deployment evidence}
measures behavior on a stated platform, configuration, workload, or fault
condition.  A planning benchmark is not evidence of robustness to network
timing, and a formal guarantee under a dynamics model is not evidence that an
implementation uses the correct frame, clock, or software version.  The
distinction matters for coordinated-autonomy studies in particular: a
mission-level probability or reliability estimate can be valid for its model and
data without certifying the runtime interfaces that execute the mission
\cite{chaki2013probabilistic,porter2013reliability}.
